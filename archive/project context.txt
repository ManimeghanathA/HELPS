project context:
HELPSs (Helicopter Emergency Landing Place Suggestion System)
Internal System Design Document (Version 1.0)

Document Purpose

This document serves as the internal architecture and design reference for the HELPSs project. It captures the design philosophy, system architecture, feature engineering pipeline, and reasoning behind every major decision taken during the conceptualization of the system. It is intended for the project team, mentors, and future developers to ensure that implementation remains aligned with the original vision.

1. Introduction

Emergency landing is one of the most critical situations a helicopter pilot can face. During an engine failure or any emergency requiring immediate landing, the pilot often has only a few seconds to identify a suitable landing location. Traditionally, this decision relies entirely on pilot experience, visual observation, and operational judgment.

The objective of HELPSs is not to replace the pilot's decision, but rather to act as an intelligent decision-support system capable of identifying and ranking potential emergency landing opportunities within a geographical region.

Unlike conventional navigation systems, HELPSs does not search for predefined helipads. Instead, it analyzes natural terrain, identifies physically suitable landing opportunities, evaluates them against helicopter-specific operational constraints, and presents ranked recommendations to the pilot.

The fundamental philosophy of HELPSs is:

The pilot always makes the final decision. The system only assists by reducing the search space and providing explainable recommendations.

2. Design Philosophy

One of the biggest architectural decisions made during the design of HELPSs was to separate terrain understanding from helicopter evaluation.

Initially, we attempted to directly search for landing locations based on helicopter dimensions. However, this approach quickly became difficult to generalize because every helicopter has different landing requirements.

Instead, HELPSs adopts a two-stage philosophy.

The first stage focuses entirely on understanding the terrain.

The second stage evaluates that terrain according to helicopter-specific operational requirements.

This separation allows the same database to support multiple helicopter types without rebuilding the entire analysis pipeline.

The guiding philosophy therefore becomes:

Understand the Terrain

↓

Store Terrain Knowledge

↓

Evaluate According to Helicopter

↓

Recommend Landing Opportunity

This architecture also makes the system scalable, modular, and easier to maintain.

3. Pilot-Centric Thinking

Throughout the project, one important realization was that the architecture should not be driven by computer science alone.

Instead, it should answer the questions that naturally arise in a pilot's mind during an emergency.

Rather than asking

"What features can we extract?"

HELPSs asks

"What information does the pilot need to safely land?"

This changes the entire architecture.

The pilot's reasoning generally follows the following sequence:

Can I physically land there?

↓

Is the terrain safe?

↓

Can my helicopter operate there?

↓

Which option is the safest?

HELPSs is therefore designed to replicate this reasoning process computationally.

4. Overall System Architecture

The complete system consists of two independent processing pipelines.

Offline Processing Pipeline

The offline pipeline performs all computationally intensive geospatial analysis.

Its objective is to understand the terrain once and store all useful information inside a spatial database.

Since terrain changes relatively slowly, this stage can be executed periodically.

The offline pipeline includes:

Satellite Image Acquisition
DEM Acquisition
GIS Data Acquisition
Terrain Segmentation
Empty Land Detection
Feature Extraction
Database Construction

Once completed, the system possesses a comprehensive database describing every potential landing opportunity.

Online Processing Pipeline

The online pipeline operates during flight.

Instead of processing satellite imagery again, it simply queries the database.

Inputs include:

Current helicopter coordinates
Helicopter model
Pilot preferences

The system then evaluates all nearby terrain objects according to helicopter requirements and returns the best landing opportunities ranked by suitability.

This allows recommendations to be generated within seconds.

5. Core Idea of the Project

One of the biggest conceptual breakthroughs during the project was understanding that the primary task is not finding landing points.

Instead, the primary task is identifying empty terrain that has the potential to become a landing opportunity.

Everything else follows afterwards.

The pipeline therefore begins with one simple objective:

Find all regions that are physically free of permanent obstacles.

Examples include:

Grasslands
Open soil
Dry riverbeds
Open clearings
Large water bodies
Open rocky plateaus

Regions containing dense trees, buildings, towers, and other permanent obstacles are excluded immediately.

These initial obstacle-free regions become the foundation upon which every subsequent analysis is performed.

6. Empty Land Detection – The Backbone of HELPSs

The first major computational stage of HELPSs is the extraction of empty land.

This stage determines all contiguous regions that satisfy the following basic condition:

The region contains no permanent obstacle that would immediately prevent helicopter landing.

Permanent obstacles include:

Trees
Buildings
Communication towers
Electric poles
Large rock formations
Dense forests

Large water bodies are not rejected.

Instead, they are stored separately and labelled as water terrain.

This design decision ensures future compatibility with amphibious or float-equipped helicopters.

The output of this stage is a collection of obstacle-free polygons.

These polygons form the first candidate landing regions.

7. Progressive Terrain Characterization

Once empty regions are identified, HELPSs begins extracting descriptive features.

Unlike traditional filtering systems, HELPSs does not immediately reject regions based on helicopter limitations.

Instead, it builds a detailed profile of every terrain object.

These profiles include:

Geometry

The geometry of every region is computed.

Important measurements include:

Total Area
Maximum Available Area
Maximum Clear Diameter
Maximum Clear Length
Maximum Clear Width
Shape Compactness
Polygon Geometry

These parameters describe how much usable space exists inside the region.

They remain independent of helicopter type.

Terrain Characteristics

Terrain characteristics determine how suitable the land may be.

Important parameters include:

Local Slope
Maximum Slope
Average Slope
Slope Variability
Terrain Roughness

Among these, slope remains the most challenging research problem within HELPSs.

Large polygons rarely possess uniform slope.

Therefore, instead of representing slope using a single average value, HELPSs aims to preserve the spatial distribution of slope so that helicopter-specific evaluation can later identify feasible landing footprints.

This remains an active research challenge and is intentionally documented as future work within Version 1.

Surface Characteristics

The physical nature of the surface significantly affects landing feasibility.

Parameters include:

Surface Type
Surface Firmness
Vegetation Density
Loose Soil
Sand
Marsh
Rock
Water

Water surfaces are preserved rather than rejected.

Each region is labelled according to its terrain class.

Environmental Characteristics

The environment surrounding a landing region can strongly influence operational safety.

Examples include:

Distance to Trees
Distance to Buildings
Nearby Obstacles
Accessibility
Road Distance
Settlement Distance

These parameters become important during final ranking.

8. Helicopter-Specific Evaluation

Only after the terrain database has been constructed does HELPSs consider helicopter-specific information.

Every helicopter possesses unique operational characteristics.

Examples include:

Rotor Diameter
Tail Clearance
Safety Buffer
Maximum Landing Slope
Water Landing Capability
Surface Restrictions

Rather than storing helicopter-specific landing zones, HELPSs dynamically evaluates every terrain object according to the selected helicopter.

This architecture makes the database reusable across multiple helicopter platforms.

9. Recommendation Strategy

Finally, HELPSs generates recommendations.

The pilot may choose between different recommendation modes.

For example:

Nearest Suitable Landing Area

Ranks locations according to distance from the helicopter.

Safest Landing Area

Ranks locations according to terrain suitability score.

Hybrid Recommendation

Combines both operational safety and travel distance into a weighted ranking model.

The final output presented to the pilot contains:

Landing Polygon
Distance
Suitability Score
Terrain Type
Surface Type
Maximum Available Area
Estimated Landing Confidence
Explanation of Recommendation

This ensures that recommendations remain transparent and interpretable.

10. Current Open Research Problems

Although the overall architecture of HELPSs has matured significantly, several research challenges remain intentionally unsolved in Version 1.

The most significant among these is the representation of slope within large landing regions. A single average or maximum slope cannot adequately describe a large polygon where different areas exhibit different terrain characteristics. The challenge is to preserve enough spatial information so that, during online evaluation, the system can determine whether a contiguous footprint meeting a helicopter's slope limits exists.

Other open problems include automatic identification of the optimal landing footprint within large polygons, robust approach and departure path analysis using available GIS data, integration of dynamic weather conditions, detection of power lines and small obstacles from publicly available datasets, and quantifying confidence in recommendations when input data resolution is limited.

These are not shortcomings of the project but natural extensions of the research. HELPSs Version 1 focuses on building a reliable terrain-understanding and recommendation framework, while these problems provide a clear roadmap for future development.

Conclusion

HELPSs is designed as an AI-assisted geospatial decision-support system that assists helicopter pilots during emergency landings by automatically identifying, characterizing, evaluating, and ranking potential landing opportunities. The project deliberately separates terrain understanding from helicopter evaluation, allowing a single spatial database to support multiple aircraft types while remaining modular and extensible.

At its core, HELPSs does not attempt to automate pilot decision-making. Instead, it reduces cognitive load during emergencies by presenting a set of physically and operationally suitable landing opportunities, together with transparent explanations of why each recommendation is considered appropriate. This philosophy ensures that the pilot remains in command while benefiting from rapid, data-driven situational awareness.

22. Final Architecture

The final architecture we agreed upon is summarized below.

Offline Stage
Acquire satellite imagery, DEM, GIS layers, and administrative data.
Identify obstacle-free (empty) terrain by removing permanent obstacles such as buildings and trees.
Preserve terrain classes (grass, soil, water, rock, etc.) rather than forcing helicopter-specific decisions.
Characterize each terrain polygon by computing:
Geometry (area, diameter, length, width)
Slope and terrain properties
Surface characteristics
Environmental context
Accessibility metrics
Store all characterized polygons in the spatial database.
Online Stage
Receive helicopter information (model, rotor diameter, slope limit, water capability, etc.).
Retrieve nearby candidate terrain polygons.
Evaluate compatibility:
Surface compatibility
Geometric compatibility
Slope compatibility
Obstacle clearance
Rank compatible landing opportunities based on pilot preference:
Nearest suitable location
Safest location
Hybrid ranking (distance + suitability)
Present explainable recommendations, leaving the final decision to the pilot.
23. Design Philosophy (Final)

HELPSs is not a system that tells a pilot where to land.

HELPSs is a system that understands terrain, preserves that knowledge in a reusable form, and rapidly answers the pilot's question:

"Given my helicopter, what are the safest and most feasible emergency landing opportunities around me?"

The system is intentionally designed as a decision-support tool, not an autonomous decision-making system. It assists the pilot by reducing search time, providing objective terrain analysis, and presenting transparent recommendations while ensuring that the pilot remains the final authority.

Final Thoughts

This journey was valuable because we did not jump directly to implementation. We repeatedly questioned our assumptions, rejected abstractions that did not match real pilot behavior (such as isolated landing points and arbitrary grids), separated terrain understanding from helicopter evaluation, and identified the true unresolved research challenge instead of hiding it.

As of now, the project has a solid conceptual foundation. The architecture is modular, scalable, and explainable. More importantly, it remains faithful to its original objective: helping pilots make better emergency landing decisions through intelligent geospatial analysis rather than replacing their judgment.
